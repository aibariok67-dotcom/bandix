Vertical Farming

Vertical farming — the practice of growing crops in stacked layers within controlled indoor environments — has moved from a futuristic concept to a growing industry over the past decade. Proponents argue that by building upward rather than outward, cities can produce fresh produce with a fraction of the land traditionally required. In Singapore, where arable land is scarce, several vertical farms now supply supermarkets with leafy greens grown just kilometres from where they are sold, cutting transport time and reducing spoilage.

Water efficiency is often cited as one of the technology's clearest advantages: hydroponic and aeroponic systems can use up to ninety percent less water than open-field agriculture, since moisture is recirculated rather than lost to evaporation or runoff. However, the energy required to power artificial lighting and climate control remains a significant obstacle. Unlike traditional farms that rely on sunlight, vertical farms depend heavily on electricity, and in regions where that electricity comes from fossil fuels, the environmental benefits can be partially offset.

Despite these challenges, investment in the sector continues to grow, driven partly by concerns over food security in densely populated regions and partly by advances in LED technology that have lowered energy costs. Whether vertical farming can scale to feed entire cities, rather than supplement existing food systems, remains an open question among agricultural economists.
